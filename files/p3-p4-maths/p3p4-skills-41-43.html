SKILL 41

Forming Differential Equations from Worded Problems

What It Is

A differential equation (DE) is any equation that contains a derivative such as \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) or \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}\). When a real-world situation describes how one quantity changes with respect to another, you can translate that description directly into a DE.

The key phrase to look for is "rate of change". If something increases or decreases at a rate related to another quantity, that is a DE waiting to be written.

You do not need to solve the equation at this stage — forming it correctly is the entire task of Skill 41. Solving comes in Skills 42 and 43.

The Rules

Key translation table:

$$\text{"rate of change of } y \text{ with respect to } t \text{"} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dt}$$
$$\text{"} y \text{ increases at a rate proportional to } y \text{"} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dt} = ky \quad (k > 0)$$
$$\text{"} y \text{ decreases at a rate proportional to } y \text{"} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dt} = -ky \quad (k > 0)$$
$$\text{"rate proportional to the square of } y \text{"} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dt} = ky^2$$

The constant of proportionality \(k\) is always positive. A decrease is indicated by placing a negative sign in front of \(k\), not by making \(k\) negative.

Where to Apply This
  • Population problems: "The population grows at a rate proportional to its current size."
  • Radioactive decay: "The number of atoms decreases at a rate proportional to the number present."
  • Cooling problems: "The temperature drops at a rate proportional to the difference between the object and room temperature."
  • Liquid flow: "Water drains from a tank at a rate proportional to the square root of the depth."
  • Area / volume change: "A spherical balloon is inflated so that its volume increases at a constant rate."
Worked Examples
Example 1 — Population Growth

The size \(P\) of a bacteria colony at time \(t\) hours increases at a rate directly proportional to the current population. Write a differential equation for \(P\).

Step 1 — Identify what is changing and with respect to what

\(P\) is changing with respect to \(t\), so the derivative is \(\dfrac{dP}{dt}\).

Step 2 — Translate "proportional to the current population"

Directly proportional to \(P\) means the right-hand side is \(kP\) for some positive constant \(k\).

Step 3 — Decide the sign

The population increases, so no negative sign is needed.

Step 4 — Write the DE $$\boxed{\frac{dP}{dt} = kP}$$

where \(k > 0\) is a positive constant.

Example 2 — Liquid Draining from a Tank

A tank contains water to a depth of \(h\) metres. Water leaks from a hole at the bottom at a rate proportional to the square root of the depth. Write a differential equation for \(h\) in terms of time \(t\) seconds.

Step 1 — Identify the derivative

The depth \(h\) is changing with respect to time \(t\), so the derivative is \(\dfrac{dh}{dt}\).

Step 2 — Translate "proportional to the square root of the depth"

Proportional to \(\sqrt{h}\) means the right-hand side is \(k\sqrt{h}\) for some positive constant \(k\).

Step 3 — Decide the sign

The water is draining, so \(h\) is decreasing. A minus sign is required.

Step 4 — Write the DE $$\boxed{\frac{dh}{dt} = -k\sqrt{h}}$$

where \(k > 0\) is a positive constant.

Practice Questions
  • Q1 The mass \(m\) (kg) of a radioactive substance at time \(t\) years decreases at a rate proportional to the current mass. Write a DE for \(m\).
  • Q2 The number of fish \(F\) in a lake increases at a rate proportional to \(F\). Write a DE for \(F\) in terms of \(t\) weeks.
  • Q3 A cup of coffee cools at a rate proportional to the difference between its temperature \(\theta\)°C and the room temperature of 20°C. Write a DE for \(\theta\) in terms of \(t\) minutes. (Take the room temperature into account.)
  • Q4 The volume \(V\) cm³ of a spherical bubble grows at a rate proportional to its surface area \(S\) cm², where \(S = 4\pi r^2\) and \(V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\). Write a DE for \(V\) in terms of \(t\) without introducing \(r\) or \(S\) into the final answer. (Hint: express \(S\) in terms of \(V\).)
  • Q5 The depth \(h\) (m) of water in a conical tank decreases at a rate proportional to \(h^2\). Write a DE for \(h\) in terms of \(t\) seconds.
  • Q6 A population \(P\) of insects increases at a rate proportional to \(\sqrt{P}\). Write a DE for \(P\) in terms of \(t\) days.
  • Q7 A quantity \(y\) increases at a constant rate of 3 units per second plus an additional rate proportional to \(y\) itself. Write a DE for \(y\) in terms of \(t\) seconds.
Fully Worked Solutions
  • Solution Q1 — Radioactive Decay
    Derivative:

    Mass \(m\) changes with time \(t\), so we need \(\dfrac{dm}{dt}\).

    Proportionality:

    Rate proportional to \(m\) gives right-hand side \(km\).

    Sign:

    Mass is decreasing, so a minus sign is needed.

    $$\boxed{\frac{dm}{dt} = -km, \quad k > 0}$$
  • Solution Q2 — Fish Population
    Derivative:

    \(F\) changes with \(t\), so the derivative is \(\dfrac{dF}{dt}\).

    Proportionality:

    Rate proportional to \(F\) gives \(kF\).

    Sign:

    Fish are increasing, so no negative sign.

    $$\boxed{\frac{dF}{dt} = kF, \quad k > 0}$$
  • Solution Q3 — Newton's Law of Cooling
    Derivative:

    \(\theta\) changes with \(t\), so the derivative is \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\).

    Proportionality:

    Rate proportional to the excess temperature \((\theta - 20)\), giving \(k(\theta - 20)\).

    Sign:

    Coffee is cooling, so \(\theta\) decreases, requiring a minus sign.

    $$\boxed{\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -k(\theta - 20), \quad k > 0}$$
  • Solution Q4 — Spherical Bubble
    Express S in terms of V:

    From \(V = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\), we get \(r = \left(\dfrac{3V}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3}\).

    Then \(S = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi \left(\dfrac{3V}{4\pi}\right)^{2/3}\).

    This simplifies to \(S = (36\pi)^{1/3}\, V^{2/3}\).

    Write the DE:

    Rate proportional to \(S\) means \(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = kS\). Substituting:

    $$\boxed{\frac{dV}{dt} = k(36\pi)^{1/3}\, V^{2/3}}$$

    Note: Since \((36\pi)^{1/3}\) is itself a constant, this is often written simply as \(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = cV^{2/3}\) where \(c\) absorbs all constants.

  • Solution Q5 — Conical Tank
    Derivative:

    \(h\) changes with \(t\), giving \(\dfrac{dh}{dt}\).

    Proportionality and sign:

    Rate proportional to \(h^2\), and depth is decreasing.

    $$\boxed{\frac{dh}{dt} = -kh^2, \quad k > 0}$$
  • Solution Q6 — Insect Population
    Derivative:

    \(P\) changes with \(t\), giving \(\dfrac{dP}{dt}\).

    Proportionality and sign:

    Rate proportional to \(\sqrt{P}\), population is increasing.

    $$\boxed{\frac{dP}{dt} = k\sqrt{P}, \quad k > 0}$$
  • Solution Q7 — Combined Rate
    Two separate contributions to the rate:

    Constant rate of 3 units per second \(\rightarrow\) contributes \(+3\) to \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\).

    Additional rate proportional to \(y\) \(\rightarrow\) contributes \(+ky\).

    Combine:
    $$\boxed{\frac{dy}{dt} = ky + 3, \quad k > 0}$$
SKILL 42

General Solution vs Particular Solution

What It Is

Solving a differential equation by separation of variables means rearranging the equation so that all terms involving \(y\) (and \(dy\)) are on one side, and all terms involving \(x\) (or \(t\)) are on the other side. You then integrate both sides.

The general solution contains an arbitrary constant \(C\). This represents an entire family of curves — infinitely many solutions, one for each value of \(C\).

A particular solution is obtained by substituting a known point — called a boundary condition or initial condition — into the general solution to find the exact value of \(C\). This gives one specific curve.

Think of it this way: the general solution is all possible answers; the particular solution is the one answer that fits the given starting information.

The Method — Separation of Variables

For a DE of the form \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)\,g(y)\):

$$\text{Step 1: Separate} \qquad \frac{1}{g(y)}\,dy = f(x)\,dx$$
$$\text{Step 2: Integrate both sides} \qquad \int \frac{1}{g(y)}\,dy = \int f(x)\,dx + C$$
$$\text{Step 3 (if required): Apply boundary condition to find } C$$

Important: Only one constant \(C\) is needed — one from the left integral and one from the right would just combine into a single constant anyway.

Also important: When integrating \(\dfrac{1}{y}\,dy\), the result is \(\ln|y|\) — always use the modulus sign.

Where to Apply This
  • General solution only: When the question asks you to "find the general solution" or "solve the DE" without giving any additional information.
  • Particular solution: When a starting value is given (e.g., "when \(t = 0\), \(y = 5\)"), substitute it after finding the general solution.
  • Separation applies when: The DE can be written in the form \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x) \cdot g(y)\) — that is, the right side factors into a function of \(x\) times a function of \(y\).
  • Does NOT apply when: The equation cannot be separated (e.g., \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x + y\) — these require different methods not on this syllabus).
Worked Examples
Example 1 — General Solution

Find the general solution of \(\;\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 y\).

Step 1 — Separate the variables

Divide both sides by \(y\) (assuming \(y \neq 0\)) and multiply both sides by \(dx\):

$$\frac{1}{y}\,dy = 3x^2\,dx$$
Step 2 — Integrate both sides $$\int \frac{1}{y}\,dy = \int 3x^2\,dx$$ $$\ln|y| = x^3 + C$$
Step 3 — Exponentiate to make y the subject

Apply \(e^{\,\cdot\,}\) to both sides:

$$|y| = e^{x^3 + C} = e^C \cdot e^{x^3}$$

Since \(e^C\) is just a positive constant, we rename it \(A\) (where \(A > 0\), but we allow it to absorb the ± from the modulus):

$$\boxed{y = Ae^{x^3}}$$

where \(A\) is an arbitrary constant.

Example 2 — Particular Solution

Solve the differential equation \(\;\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{2x}{y}\), given that \(y = 4\) when \(x = 0\).

Step 1 — Separate the variables

Multiply both sides by \(y\) and by \(dx\):

$$y\,dy = 2x\,dx$$
Step 2 — Integrate both sides $$\int y\,dy = \int 2x\,dx$$ $$\frac{y^2}{2} = x^2 + C$$
Step 3 — This is the general solution. Now apply the boundary condition \(y = 4\) when \(x = 0\) $$\frac{4^2}{2} = 0^2 + C \implies \frac{16}{2} = C \implies C = 8$$
Step 4 — Write the particular solution $$\frac{y^2}{2} = x^2 + 8$$ $$\boxed{y^2 = 2x^2 + 16}$$

(Or equivalently, \(y = \sqrt{2x^2+16}\) since \(y = 4 > 0\) at \(x = 0\).)

Practice Questions
  • Q1 Find the general solution of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 5y\).
  • Q2 Find the general solution of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x^2}{y^3}\).
  • Q3 Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -2xy\), given that \(y = 3\) when \(x = 0\). Give the particular solution in the form \(y = f(x)\).
  • Q4 Find the general solution of \(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = -kV\), where \(k\) is a positive constant. (Leave \(k\) in your answer.)
  • Q5 Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{4x}{y^2}\), given that \(y = 2\) when \(x = 1\). Give your answer in the form \(y^3 = f(x)\).
  • Q6 Find the particular solution of \(\dfrac{dN}{dt} = N^2\), given that \(N = 1\) when \(t = 0\). (State the domain of validity.)
  • Q7 Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{\sin x}{y}\), given that \(y = 0\) when \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\). Give your answer in the form \(y^2 = f(x)\).
Fully Worked Solutions
  • Solution Q1
    Separate: \(\dfrac{1}{y}\,dy = 5\,dx\)
    Integrate: \(\ln|y| = 5x + C\)
    Exponentiate: \(|y| = e^{5x+C} = e^C e^{5x}\)
    $$\boxed{y = Ae^{5x}}$$

    where \(A\) is an arbitrary constant.

  • Solution Q2
    Separate: \(y^3\,dy = x^2\,dx\)
    Integrate: $$\int y^3\,dy = \int x^2\,dx \implies \frac{y^4}{4} = \frac{x^3}{3} + C$$
    $$\boxed{y^4 = \frac{4x^3}{3} + A}$$

    where \(A = 4C\) is an arbitrary constant.

  • Solution Q3
    Separate: \(\dfrac{1}{y}\,dy = -2x\,dx\)
    Integrate: \(\ln|y| = -x^2 + C\)
    General solution: \(y = Ae^{-x^2}\)
    Apply \(y = 3\) when \(x = 0\): \(3 = Ae^{0} = A\), so \(A = 3\)
    $$\boxed{y = 3e^{-x^2}}$$
  • Solution Q4
    Separate: \(\dfrac{1}{V}\,dV = -k\,dt\)
    Integrate: \(\ln|V| = -kt + C\)
    Exponentiate: \(V = Ae^{-kt}\)
    $$\boxed{V = Ae^{-kt}}$$

    where \(A\) is an arbitrary constant and \(k > 0\).

  • Solution Q5
    Separate: \(y^2\,dy = 4x\,dx\)
    Integrate: $$\frac{y^3}{3} = 2x^2 + C \implies y^3 = 6x^2 + A$$
    Apply \(y = 2\) when \(x = 1\): $$8 = 6(1) + A \implies A = 2$$
    $$\boxed{y^3 = 6x^2 + 2}$$
  • Solution Q6
    Separate: \(\dfrac{1}{N^2}\,dN = dt\), i.e. \(N^{-2}\,dN = dt\)
    Integrate: $$-N^{-1} = t + C \implies -\frac{1}{N} = t + C$$
    Apply \(N = 1\) when \(t = 0\): $$-\frac{1}{1} = 0 + C \implies C = -1$$
    Substitute \(C = -1\) and rearrange: $$-\frac{1}{N} = t - 1 \implies N = \frac{1}{1-t}$$
    $$\boxed{N = \frac{1}{1-t}, \quad t < 1}$$

    Valid for \(t < 1\) only; \(N \to \infty\) as \(t \to 1^-\).

  • Solution Q7
    Separate: \(y\,dy = \sin x\,dx\)
    Integrate: $$\frac{y^2}{2} = -\cos x + C \implies y^2 = -2\cos x + A$$
    Apply \(y = 0\) when \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\): $$0 = -2\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) + A = -2(0) + A \implies A = 0$$
    $$\boxed{y^2 = -2\cos x}$$
SKILL 43

Modelling: Growth, Decay and Newton's Law of Cooling

What It Is

Skill 43 brings everything together: you take a real-world scenario, form the DE (Skill 41), solve it by separation of variables (Skill 42), then interpret the answer in context.

Exponential growth describes quantities that increase at a rate proportional to themselves — bacteria, compound interest, early-stage viral spread.

Exponential decay describes quantities that decrease at a rate proportional to themselves — radioactive material, drug concentration in blood, charge on a capacitor.

Newton's Law of Cooling describes how an object's temperature approaches room (ambient) temperature at a rate proportional to the difference between the object's temperature and the room temperature. It produces a shifted exponential — the temperature approaches a non-zero limit.

In every case, the key skill is correctly identifying the initial condition to find the particular solution, and then using that solution to answer questions about specific times or values.

The Three Core Models

Model 1 — Exponential Growth

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = ky \;\xrightarrow{\text{solve}}\; \boxed{y = Ae^{kt}} \quad (k > 0)$$

Model 2 — Exponential Decay

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = -ky \;\xrightarrow{\text{solve}}\; \boxed{y = Ae^{-kt}} \quad (k > 0)$$

Model 3 — Newton's Law of Cooling

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -k(\theta - \theta_0) \;\xrightarrow{\text{solve}}\; \boxed{\theta = \theta_0 + Ae^{-kt}} \quad (k > 0)$$

where \(\theta_0\) is the constant ambient (room) temperature.

In every case: \(A\) is determined by the initial condition (usually the value of \(y\) or \(\theta\) at \(t = 0\)).

Where to Apply This
  • Use Model 1 (growth) when the quantity increases and "rate of change is proportional to current value" — population, investment value.
  • Use Model 2 (decay) when the quantity decreases and "rate of change is proportional to current value" — radioactive decay, drug clearance, value depreciation.
  • Use Model 3 (cooling) when temperature decreases toward a fixed ambient level — hot object in a room, cooling of lava, warming of a cold drink.
  • Finding time: Substitute the target value of \(y\) into the particular solution and use \(\ln\) to solve for \(t\).
  • Finding value at time \(t\): Simply substitute \(t\) into the particular solution and evaluate.
Worked Examples
Example 1 — Radioactive Decay

A radioactive substance has mass \(m\) grams at time \(t\) years. The substance decays at a rate proportional to its current mass, with constant of proportionality \(k = 0.03\).

Initially, \(m = 200\) g. Find: (a) the mass after 10 years, (b) the time taken for the mass to halve.

Step 1 — Form the DE $$\frac{dm}{dt} = -0.03m$$
Step 2 — Separate and integrate $$\frac{1}{m}\,dm = -0.03\,dt$$ $$\ln|m| = -0.03t + C$$ $$m = Ae^{-0.03t}$$
Step 3 — Apply initial condition: \(m = 200\) when \(t = 0\) $$200 = Ae^{0} = A \implies A = 200$$

Particular solution: \(m = 200e^{-0.03t}\)

Part (a): Mass after 10 years $$m = 200e^{-0.03 \times 10} = 200e^{-0.3} \approx 200 \times 0.7408 \approx 148.2 \text{ g}$$ $$\boxed{m \approx 148.2 \text{ g}}$$
Part (b): Time to halve (i.e., find \(t\) when \(m = 100\)) $$100 = 200e^{-0.03t}$$ $$\frac{1}{2} = e^{-0.03t}$$ $$\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -0.03t$$ $$-\ln 2 = -0.03t$$ $$t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.03} = \frac{0.6931\ldots}{0.03} \approx 23.1 \text{ years}$$ $$\boxed{t \approx 23.1 \text{ years}}$$
Example 2 — Newton's Law of Cooling

A cup of tea is made at 95°C and placed in a room at 20°C. After 5 minutes the tea has cooled to 75°C. Find the temperature of the tea after a further 10 minutes (i.e., at \(t = 15\) min).

Step 1 — Form the DE (Newton's Law of Cooling) $$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = -k(\theta - 20)$$
Step 2 — Solve by separation

Let \(u = \theta - 20\), so \(\dfrac{du}{dt} = \dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\). Then:

$$\frac{du}{u} = -k\,dt \implies \ln|u| = -kt + C \implies u = Ae^{-kt}$$ $$\theta - 20 = Ae^{-kt} \implies \theta = 20 + Ae^{-kt}$$
Step 3 — Apply initial condition: \(\theta = 95\) when \(t = 0\) $$95 = 20 + Ae^{0} = 20 + A \implies A = 75$$

So: \(\theta = 20 + 75e^{-kt}\)

Step 4 — Use second condition (\(\theta = 75\) when \(t = 5\)) to find \(k\) $$75 = 20 + 75e^{-5k}$$ $$55 = 75e^{-5k}$$ $$e^{-5k} = \frac{55}{75} = \frac{11}{15}$$ $$-5k = \ln\!\left(\frac{11}{15}\right) \implies k = -\frac{1}{5}\ln\!\left(\frac{11}{15}\right) = \frac{1}{5}\ln\!\left(\frac{15}{11}\right)$$
Step 5 — Find \(\theta\) at \(t = 15\)

It is cleaner to use \(e^{-5k} = \dfrac{11}{15}\), so \(e^{-15k} = \left(\dfrac{11}{15}\right)^3\).

$$\theta = 20 + 75\left(\frac{11}{15}\right)^3 = 20 + 75 \times \frac{1331}{3375} = 20 + \frac{99825}{3375}$$ $$= 20 + 29.578\ldots \approx 49.6°\text{C}$$ $$\boxed{\theta \approx 49.6°\text{C}}$$

  • Q1 A population of bacteria grows so that \(\dfrac{dP}{dt} = 0.05P\), where \(P\) is the number of bacteria at time \(t\) hours. Initially \(P = 500\). Find: (a) the population after 8 hours; (b) the time for the population to reach 2000.
  • Q2 The mass \(m\) (grams) of a radioactive isotope satisfies \(\dfrac{dm}{dt} = -0.012m\). Initially \(m = 80\) g. Find (a) the mass after 20 years; (b) the half-life of the isotope (time for mass to halve).
  • Q3 A hot object cools according to \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = -k(\theta - 15)\), where \(\theta\)°C is the temperature and the room temperature is 15°C. Initially \(\theta = 80\)°C. After 4 minutes, \(\theta = 60\)°C. Find the temperature after 12 minutes.
  • Q4 The value \(V\) (£) of a car satisfies \(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = -0.25V\), where \(t\) is years since purchase. Initially the car is worth £24,000. Find (a) its value after 3 years; (b) the time for the car to be worth £6,000.
  • Q5 A cold drink at 4°C is placed in a room at 22°C. After 10 minutes it has warmed to 10°C. Using Newton's Law of Cooling/Warming, find the temperature at \(t = 30\) minutes. [The same model applies — the constant \(k\) is still positive even though the object warms.]
  • Q6 The number of people \(N\) infected with a virus satisfies \(\dfrac{dN}{dt} = kN\). On day 0, \(N = 50\). On day 3, \(N = 200\). Find (a) the value of \(k\); (b) the predicted number of infected people on day 7 (nearest whole number).
  • Q7 A body found at a crime scene has a temperature of 30°C. Room temperature is 18°C. Normal body temperature is 37°C. Given \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = -k(\theta - 18)\) with \(k = 0.1\) per hour, how long ago (in hours) did death occur? Give your answer to 2 decimal places.
Fully Worked Solutions
  • Solution Q1 — Bacteria Growth
    Solve the DE:

    Separate and integrate: \(\ln P = 0.05t + C \implies P = Ae^{0.05t}\).

    Initial condition \(P = 500\) at \(t = 0\): \(A = 500\).

    Particular solution: \(P = 500e^{0.05t}\).

    Part (a): \(t = 8\) $$P = 500e^{0.05 \times 8} = 500e^{0.4} \approx 500 \times 1.4918 \approx 745.9$$
    $$\boxed{P \approx 746 \text{ bacteria (to nearest whole number)}}$$
    Part (b): Find \(t\) when \(P = 2000\) $$2000 = 500e^{0.05t} \implies 4 = e^{0.05t} \implies \ln 4 = 0.05t \implies t = \frac{\ln 4}{0.05} = \frac{1.3863}{0.05}$$
    $$\boxed{t \approx 27.7 \text{ hours}}$$
  • Solution Q2 — Radioactive Isotope
    Solve the DE:

    Particular solution: \(m = 80e^{-0.012t}\).

    Part (a): \(t = 20\) $$m = 80e^{-0.012 \times 20} = 80e^{-0.24} \approx 80 \times 0.7866 \approx 62.9$$
    $$\boxed{m \approx 62.9 \text{ g}}$$
    Part (b): Half-life (find \(t\) when \(m = 40\)) $$40 = 80e^{-0.012t} \implies \tfrac{1}{2} = e^{-0.012t} \implies \ln\tfrac{1}{2} = -0.012t \implies t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.012}$$
    $$\boxed{t \approx 57.8 \text{ years}}$$
  • Solution Q3 — Newton's Law of Cooling
    General solution:

    \(\theta = 15 + Ae^{-kt}\).

    Initial condition \(\theta = 80\) at \(t = 0\):

    \(80 = 15 + A \implies A = 65\). So \(\theta = 15 + 65e^{-kt}\).

    Use \(\theta = 60\) at \(t = 4\) to find \(k\): $$60 = 15 + 65e^{-4k} \implies 45 = 65e^{-4k} \implies e^{-4k} = \frac{45}{65} = \frac{9}{13}$$ $$k = -\frac{1}{4}\ln\!\left(\frac{9}{13}\right) = \frac{1}{4}\ln\!\left(\frac{13}{9}\right)$$
    Find \(\theta\) at \(t = 12\): Use \(e^{-12k} = \left(\frac{9}{13}\right)^3\) $$\theta = 15 + 65\left(\frac{9}{13}\right)^3 = 15 + 65 \times \frac{729}{2197} = 15 + \frac{47385}{2197} \approx 15 + 21.57 \approx 36.6°\text{C}$$
    $$\boxed{\theta \approx 36.6°\text{C}}$$
  • Solution Q4 — Car Depreciation
    Particular solution:

    \(V = 24000\, e^{-0.25t}\).

    Part (a): \(t = 3\) $$V = 24000\, e^{-0.75} \approx 24000 \times 0.4724 \approx 11337$$
    $$\boxed{V \approx £11{,}337}$$
    Part (b): Find \(t\) when \(V = 6000\) $$6000 = 24000\,e^{-0.25t} \implies \frac{1}{4} = e^{-0.25t} \implies \ln\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right) = -0.25t \implies t = \frac{\ln 4}{0.25}$$
    $$\boxed{t \approx 5.55 \text{ years}}$$
  • Solution Q5 — Warming Drink
    Form the DE:

    \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = -k(\theta - 22)\). (Here the drink is below room temp, so \(\theta - 22 < 0\), which makes \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} > 0\) — the drink warms up. The model is self-consistent.)

    General solution:

    \(\theta = 22 + Ae^{-kt}\).

    Initial condition \(\theta = 4\) at \(t = 0\):

    \(4 = 22 + A \implies A = -18\). So \(\theta = 22 - 18e^{-kt}\).

    Use \(\theta = 10\) at \(t = 10\) to find \(k\): $$10 = 22 - 18e^{-10k} \implies 18e^{-10k} = 12 \implies e^{-10k} = \frac{2}{3}$$ $$k = -\frac{1}{10}\ln\!\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{10}\ln\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)$$
    Find \(\theta\) at \(t = 30\): Use \(e^{-30k} = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \frac{8}{27}\) $$\theta = 22 - 18 \times \frac{8}{27} = 22 - \frac{144}{27} = 22 - \frac{16}{3} = 22 - 5.333\ldots \approx 16.67°\text{C}$$
    $$\boxed{\theta \approx 16.7°\text{C}}$$
  • Solution Q6 — Viral Spread
    Particular solution:

    \(N = 50e^{kt}\).

    Part (a): Use \(N = 200\) at \(t = 3\) to find \(k\) $$200 = 50e^{3k} \implies 4 = e^{3k} \implies 3k = \ln 4 \implies k = \frac{\ln 4}{3}$$
    $$\boxed{k = \frac{\ln 4}{3} \approx 0.4621}$$
    Part (b): Find \(N\) at \(t = 7\) $$N = 50e^{7k} = 50 \times 4^{7/3} = 50 \times 4^{7/3}$$

    \(4^{7/3} = (4^7)^{1/3} = (16384)^{1/3} \approx 25.398\)

    $$N \approx 50 \times 25.398 \approx 1270$$
    $$\boxed{N \approx 1270 \text{ people}}$$
  • Solution Q7 — Time of Death
    Set up the model:

    The body started at \(\theta = 37\)°C (normal body temperature) at the time of death (call this \(t = 0\)).

    General solution: \(\theta = 18 + Ae^{-0.1t}\).

    Apply \(\theta = 37\) at \(t = 0\): $$37 = 18 + A \implies A = 19$$

    So \(\theta = 18 + 19e^{-0.1t}\).

    Find \(t\) when \(\theta = 30\): $$30 = 18 + 19e^{-0.1t} \implies 12 = 19e^{-0.1t} \implies e^{-0.1t} = \frac{12}{19}$$ $$-0.1t = \ln\!\left(\frac{12}{19}\right) \implies t = \frac{-1}{0.1}\ln\!\left(\frac{12}{19}\right) = 10\ln\!\left(\frac{19}{12}\right)$$ $$t = 10 \times \ln(1.5833\ldots) = 10 \times 0.45985\ldots \approx 4.60$$
    $$\boxed{t \approx 4.60 \text{ hours ago}}$$
